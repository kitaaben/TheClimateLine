Renewable Energy: Powering a Sustainable Future

May 19, 2026 · 8 min read · The Climate Line

Fossil fuels built the modern world — but their side effects are pushing our planet to the breaking point. The good news? The alternatives are ready, they're affordable, and they're scaling faster than almost anyone predicted.

Solar Power: The Star of the Show

Solar photovoltaic (PV) technology has seen costs drop by more than 90% over the past two decades. Today, solar is the cheapest source of electricity in history in many parts of the world, according to the International Energy Agency (IEA).

Innovations are accelerating: perovskite solar cells promise even higher efficiencies, floating solar farms can turn reservoirs into power plants, and building-integrated photovoltaics turn windows and facades into energy generators.

Solar Cost Declines and Global Deployment

The cost of solar PV modules has fallen from $100 per watt in the 1970s to less than $0.10 per watt today — a 99.9 percent reduction. This has unlocked explosive growth: global solar capacity has grown from 40 gigawatts in 2010 to over 1,200 GW in 2025, and the IEA projects it will exceed 2,000 GW by 2028. China alone installed more than 300 GW of solar in 2025, equivalent to adding the entire power plant capacity of France in a single year. In sunny regions like the Middle East and Australia, solar is now producing electricity at a cost of less than $0.02 per kilowatt-hour — cheaper than any other form of power generation in history. World Bank programs have helped bring off-grid solar to hundreds of millions of people in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia who previously lacked access to electricity.

Next-Generation Solar Technologies

While conventional silicon solar panels have reached efficiencies of 22 to 24 percent, next-generation technologies promise to push much higher. Perovskite-silicon tandem cells, which layer a perovskite material on top of silicon, have achieved 33 percent efficiency in commercial prototypes. If they reach mass production, they could make solar power even cheaper and more land-efficient. Building-integrated photovoltaics embed solar cells into roofing tiles, glass facades, and windows, turning buildings into power plants without requiring additional land. Floating solar farms, which install panels on reservoirs and lakes, avoid land-use conflicts and benefit from the cooling effect of water, which improves efficiency. A study from Nature estimated that covering just 1 percent of the world's artificial reservoirs with floating solar could generate 400 GW of electricity.

Solar by the Numbers

Global solar capacity has grown from 40 GW in 2010 to over 1,200 GW in 2025. China, the US, and India lead deployment, but solar is booming on every continent.

Wind Energy: Harnessing the Skies

Wind turbines have grown dramatically — both onshore and offshore. A single modern offshore turbine can generate enough electricity to power thousands of homes. Offshore wind, in particular, is experiencing a renaissance, with floating turbines opening up deeper waters.

Countries like Denmark, the UK, and Germany already get significant portions of their electricity from wind. The potential is enormous: the global wind resource could theoretically meet the world's electricity demand many times over.

Challenges remain — intermittency, visual impact, and wildlife concerns — but careful siting and improved technology are steadily addressing them.

Offshore Wind: The Next Frontier

Offshore wind is experiencing remarkable growth, with global capacity expected to reach 250 GW by 2030. Fixed-bottom turbines are now cost-effective in water depths up to 60 meters, while floating turbines — anchored to the seabed with mooring lines — are opening up deeper waters where wind speeds are higher and more consistent. The UK, China, and Denmark lead in offshore wind deployment, but the United States is rapidly catching up, with major projects off the coasts of Massachusetts, New York, and California. A single 15-megawatt offshore turbine can generate enough electricity to power 12,000 European homes. The IEA estimates that offshore wind has the technical potential to meet the world's total electricity demand 11 times over, making it one of the most abundant energy resources on the planet.

Onshore Wind: Costs, Siting, and Community Benefits

Onshore wind remains the backbone of the wind industry, accounting for more than 90 percent of installed capacity. The cost of onshore wind has fallen by 70 percent since 2010, making it competitive with fossil fuels in most markets. Modern turbines have rotor diameters exceeding 150 meters, allowing them to capture more energy at lower wind speeds. Wind farms also provide significant economic benefits to rural communities: landowners receive lease payments, local governments collect tax revenue, and construction creates jobs. The World Bank has supported wind energy projects in more than 30 developing countries, helping to expand access to clean, affordable electricity while reducing dependence on imported fossil fuels.

Hydropower: The Veteran

Hydropower has been generating clean electricity for over a century. It provides more than 15% of the world's electricity — more than all other renewables combined (excluding solar and wind). Pumped-storage hydropower also serves as the world's largest battery, storing energy for when it's needed.

But large dams come with environmental and social costs: habitat disruption, methane emissions from reservoirs, and displacement of communities. The future of hydropower lies in smaller run-of-river projects and retrofitting existing dams with generation capacity.

Pumped Storage: The Original Grid Battery

Pumped-storage hydropower accounts for more than 95 percent of global energy storage capacity. It works by pumping water uphill to a reservoir when electricity is cheap or abundant, then releasing it through turbines to generate power when demand peaks. The round-trip efficiency is 70 to 80 percent, and the technology is mature, with projects operating for 50 years or more. China is building the world's largest pumped-storage facility, the Fengning station, with 3.6 GW of capacity. As variable renewables like solar and wind grow, pumped storage is becoming increasingly valuable for grid stability. The IEA estimates that global pumped-storage capacity needs to double by 2050 to support a net-zero energy system.

Run-of-River and Small Hydropower

Small hydropower projects — defined as installations with capacity below 10 megawatts — offer many of the benefits of large dams with fewer environmental costs. Run-of-river projects divert a portion of a river's flow through a turbine without creating a large reservoir, minimizing habitat disruption and methane emissions. These projects are particularly well-suited to mountainous regions in South America, Central Asia, and East Africa. The United Nations Environment Programme estimates that small hydropower could provide an additional 100 GW of clean electricity globally, much of it in regions that currently lack reliable grid access.

"Renewable energy is no longer a niche technology — it's the backbone of the 21st-century economy."

Emerging Technologies

  • Green Hydrogen: Produced by splitting water using renewable electricity. Can decarbonize industries like steelmaking, shipping, and aviation that are hard to electrify directly.
  • Geothermal Energy: Tapping into the Earth's internal heat. Enhanced geothermal systems could unlock this resource almost anywhere.
  • Wave and Tidal Power: The ocean's immense energy remains largely untapped, but new turbine designs are making progress.

Green Hydrogen: Decarbonizing Heavy Industry

Green hydrogen is produced through electrolysis, using renewable electricity to split water molecules into hydrogen and oxygen. Unlike gray hydrogen, which is made from natural gas and emits CO₂, green hydrogen produces no direct emissions. It can be used as a clean fuel for heavy industry (steelmaking, cement, chemicals), long-distance shipping, aviation, and seasonal energy storage. More than 40 countries have published national hydrogen strategies, and global investment in hydrogen projects surpassed $50 billion in 2025. The IEA projects that green hydrogen could meet 10 percent of global energy demand by 2050, making it a critical complement to direct electrification.

Geothermal and Ocean Energy

Enhanced geothermal systems (EGS) use drilling and injection technology to extract heat from hot rock formations deep underground, potentially unlocking geothermal energy almost anywhere. The US Department of Energy estimates that EGS could provide 100 GW of clean, baseload power in the United States alone by 2050. Ocean energy — wave and tidal power — remains less mature but offers enormous potential. The theoretical global wave energy resource is estimated at 30,000 terawatt-hours per year, roughly equal to global electricity consumption. The first commercial tidal stream arrays are now operating in Scotland and France, and costs are expected to fall as deployment scales up. Nature has featured research showing that combining multiple renewable sources — solar, wind, geothermal, and ocean — can create a more resilient and reliable clean energy system than relying on any single technology.

Energy Storage: The Missing Piece

The biggest criticism of renewables has always been intermittency — the sun doesn't always shine, and the wind doesn't always blow. But battery technology is closing the gap. Lithium-ion battery costs have fallen by 80% since 2015. Grid-scale battery installations are growing exponentially. And new technologies — solid-state batteries, flow batteries, iron-air batteries — promise even cheaper, longer-duration storage.

Combined with smart grids, demand response, and improved forecasting, a 100% renewable grid is no longer a dream — it's an engineering plan that multiple studies have shown is achievable by mid-century.

Lithium-Ion and Grid-Scale Batteries

Lithium-ion battery pack costs have fallen from $1,100 per kilowatt-hour in 2010 to below $120 per kWh in 2025, making utility-scale battery storage economically viable. Grid-scale battery installations grew by 120 percent globally in 2025, with total installed capacity exceeding 100 GW. Projects like the Hornsdale Power Reserve in South Australia, which uses Tesla batteries, have demonstrated that batteries can provide grid stability services faster and more cheaply than traditional gas peaker plants. The IEA estimates that global battery storage capacity needs to reach 1,000 GW by 2050 to support a net-zero energy system, a target that appears achievable given current growth rates.

Long-Duration Storage: Iron-Air and Flow Batteries

While lithium-ion batteries are ideal for short-duration storage (4 to 8 hours), longer-duration storage (100+ hours) requires different chemistries. Iron-air batteries, which use the reversible rusting of iron to store energy, are beginning to scale commercially. Form Energy, a US startup, is building a 1 GW iron-air battery plant in West Virginia that will be capable of delivering power for 100 continuous hours at a projected cost of $20 per kWh — cheap enough to enable seasonal energy storage. Vanadium flow batteries, which use liquid electrolytes stored in external tanks, offer unlimited cycle life and are already deployed in dozens of grid-scale projects in China and Japan. These technologies, combined with pumped-storage hydropower, green hydrogen, and demand-side management, make 100 percent renewable grids technically and economically feasible.

The Bottom Line

The renewable energy transition is not just about saving the planet — it's about building a better economy. Renewable jobs now outnumber fossil fuel jobs in many countries. Energy independence becomes possible when nations harness their own sun and wind. And air quality improves, saving millions of lives lost annually to fossil fuel pollution.

The technology is here. The economics work. Now it's a matter of political will and public support.

Economic Benefits and Job Creation

Renewable energy is a powerful engine of job creation. The International Energy Agency reports that the clean energy sector employed nearly 15 million people globally in 2025, with solar PV alone accounting for 5 million jobs. These jobs are distributed across manufacturing, installation, maintenance, and grid integration, and they tend to be localized — a wind turbine technician, a solar installer, or a grid engineer works in a specific community, creating economic multiplier effects. The IMF has noted that the clean energy transition could add 2 percent to global GDP by 2030, driven by increased investment, lower energy costs, and avoided damages from climate change. Meanwhile, the health benefits of reduced air pollution — cleaner air from fewer fossil fuel power plants — save an estimated 3 million lives per year globally, according to the World Bank.

Overcoming Political and Policy Barriers

The biggest obstacle to the renewable energy transition is no longer technological or economic — it is political. Fossil fuel subsidies totaled $7 trillion in 2025, according to the IMF, far exceeding the subsidies and incentives available for clean energy. These subsidies distort markets, keeping fossil fuel prices artificially low and slowing the transition. Reforming or redirecting these subsidies toward clean energy would accelerate deployment dramatically. Carbon pricing mechanisms, which put a price on CO₂ emissions, have been adopted in more than 60 national and regional jurisdictions, covering 25 percent of global emissions. The European Union's Emissions Trading System, the world's largest carbon market, rose to over $100 per ton in 2025, creating a strong economic signal for clean energy investment. With the right policies in place, the remaining barriers to a 100 percent clean energy system can be overcome.
